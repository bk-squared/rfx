A dipole 9 mm above a 30 × 30 mm ground plate has a broad beam: 7.4 dBi toward
boresight at 10 GHz. A flat lens in front of it narrows the beam when its
refractive index is highest on the axis and falls toward the rim, so that the
central rays are delayed more than the edge rays and the phase front leaving the
lens is flat. Here the lens is 90 × 90 × 30 mm, 45 mm above the dipole, and its
permittivity is limited to what PLA at variable infill can print, 1 ≤ εr ≤ 2.7,
in 3 mm pixels: 9,000 pixels, and 2,250 free variables after the feed's two
mirror symmetries. Adam raises the boresight directivity in dB averaged over
9.5, 10 and 10.5 GHz, with the gradient taken through the FDTD solve and the
near-to-far-field transform. The case shows that this gradient is enough to
shape a printable lens from a uniform block, pixel by pixel.

The mesh is graded so that the cells at the ground plate's four edges are cubes.
A metal sheet's free edge is solved about 0.35 of a cell beyond its last mesh
node ([Model on the grid](/rfx/check/model-on-grid/)). With these cubic edge
cells and their graded neighbours that offset was measured, and cross-checked
with openEMS; the plate is solved at 30.00 mm to within 0.01 mm on all three
meshes used below (measured; record kept with the project). Close to the four vertical
planes through the plate's edges the graded cells no longer line up with the
lens's 3 mm pixels, so some cells straddle two pixels; such a cell takes the permittivity of the pixel that
holds its centre.

## What happened

At the start every pixel has εr = 1.85, the middle of the printable range. This
uniform block gives 12.5 / 12.1 / 12.3 dBi at 9.5 / 10 / 10.5 GHz on the 1.0 mm
mesh the optimiser runs on. The gradient at the start already says where to
change: raising the permittivity near the axis raises the boresight
directivity, and raising it toward the corners lowers it.

The descent follows that gradient. On the 1.0 mm mesh, in the first 10
iterations the pixels on the axis rise from εr 1.85 to about 2.24, the corner
pixels fall to about 1.47, and the directivity averaged over the three
frequencies rises from 12.3 to 17.2 dBi. By iteration 30 the axis holds 2.51 to
2.56 and the corners 1.12 to 1.20, at 18.3 dBi; the remaining 120 iterations add
0.5 dB. In the final design, iteration 150, the axis pixels hold 2.56 to 2.66 in
all ten layers and the corner pixels 1.03 to 1.09. Across the lens εr spans 1.03
to 2.68, and no pixel reaches either limit of the printable range. Every layer
has a central region near the highest value; most layers have a ring of lower
permittivity around it, and the layer nearest the feed has a second,
high-permittivity ring outside that.

## The result on three meshes

Solved again without the optimiser on 1.0, 0.75 and 0.5 mm meshes, the designed
lens gives 18.5 / 19.0 / 18.8 dBi at 9.5 / 10 / 10.5 GHz on the 0.5 mm mesh. At
each of the 21 frequencies from 9 to 11 GHz the value falls as the mesh is
refined and converges by the rule below: it changes by at most 0.048 dB from
1.0 to 0.75 mm and by at most 0.034 dB from 0.75 to 0.5 mm. The feed alone gives
7.5 / 7.4 / 7.3 dBi, so the lens adds 11.0 / 11.5 / 11.5 dB. The uniform-aperture
bound 4πA/λ² for the lens's 90 × 90 mm face, the directivity of a uniformly
illuminated aperture of that size, is 20.1 / 20.5 / 21.0 dBi.

The figure below sets the designed lens against two lenses made without the
gradient. The textbook lens gives the straight ray from the dipole through each
pixel column the same optical path: n(r) = √2.7 − (√(F² + r²) − F)/t, with r
the pixel's distance from the axis, F = 45 mm the distance from the dipole to
the lens and t = 30 mm its thickness; εr = n², limited to 1–2.7, is the same in
every layer. The best uniform block is the best of three uniform blocks (εr 1,
1.85 and 2.7) by the same averaged directivity on the 1.0 mm mesh; it is
εr 2.7. Over 9 to 11 GHz on the 0.5 mm mesh the designed lens gives 1.4 to
2.1 dB more than the textbook lens and 2.1 to 5.8 dB more than the best uniform
block.

## How it was checked

The checks and their limits were written down before the design runs. They
compare rfx with itself: on three meshes, against finite differences and
against a longer record. No other solver or closed form is the reference for
these directivities.

- **Before each solve**, rfx's preflight found no error in the model, for every
  design and mesh on this page.
- **End of the simulation**: for every design and mesh on this page, the
  response still arriving after the record ends changes each frequency value
  read by at most 3.3×10⁻⁴ in amplitude (the limit is 10⁻², −40 dB). For the best
  uniform block the check could not decide with the record length the other
  designs use, because its two estimates of the late response disagreed at a
  few frequencies. That block was solved again with a record twice as long, on
  which the check passes, and its curves come from that run.
- **Three meshes**: with d₁ the change from 1.0 to 0.75 mm and d₂ the change
  from 0.75 to 0.5 mm, a value converges at a frequency when |d₂| is at most
  0.01 dB, or when d₁ and d₂ have the same sign and |d₂| is at most
  1.41 |d₁| + 0.01 dB and at most 0.3 dB. For a beamwidth the two limits are
  0.01° and 1°; for the fan design's largest directivity far from boresight
  (below) the second limit is 1 dB. An error that falls as any positive power of
  the cell size gives d₁ and d₂ the same sign and |d₂| ≤ 1.41 |d₁| on these
  meshes, so a sign change or a larger ratio is not convergence. All four
  boresight curves and the fan design's four quantities below converge at all
  21 frequencies.
- **Gradient against central differences**, at the start of each design, with a
  step of 0.05 in one pixel's εr: the relative difference is at most 1.1×10⁻³ on
  the two pixels checked for the boresight design and 2.1×10⁻⁴ on the three
  checked for the fan design (the limit is 5×10⁻²).
- **Gradient against a 1.5× longer record**: the relative difference of the
  whole gradient is 5.2×10⁻⁷ at the start and 7.7×10⁻⁵ at the final boresight
  design, and 1.2×10⁻⁶ and 5.3×10⁻⁶ for the fan design (the limit is 5×10⁻²).

An earlier version of this page used a uniform mesh, on which the plate was
solved at 30.70 mm instead of 30.00 mm. Solved on both meshes at 1.0 mm, the
earlier designs give 0.038 to 0.056 dB less boresight directivity on the graded
mesh, and the feed alone 0.071 to 0.075 dB less. How much of that comes from the
mesh and how much from the plate size was not separated.

## A second goal: a fan beam

The same block, feed and permittivity range can serve another pattern. The
second design asks for a fan beam: wide in the plane perpendicular to the dipole
and narrow in the plane that contains it. The strip is the 78 far-field
directions up to 30° from boresight whose azimuth lies within 5° of the wide
plane. The descent lowers the spread of the directivity over the strip (largest
minus smallest, in dB) minus its smallest value, averaged over 9.5, 10 and
10.5 GHz; 150 iterations start from the same uniform block.

On the 0.5 mm mesh at 9.5 / 10 / 10.5 GHz the directivity over the strip lies
between 9.7 / 10.3 / 10.4 dBi and 10.9 / 11.0 / 11.2 dBi, a spread of
1.2 / 0.8 / 0.7 dB. The half-power beamwidth in the narrow plane is
27.8° / 27.0° / 25.3°, and the largest directivity 60° or more from boresight
is 5.6 / 7.4 / 8.8 dBi. The smallest and largest strip values, the beamwidth and
the largest value far from boresight each converge over the three meshes at all
21 frequencies. The targets set before the design were a spread of at most
2 dB, a smallest strip value of at least 11.0 dBi and a narrow-plane beamwidth
of at most 23°. The design meets the spread target at the three design
frequencies. Its smallest strip value is 0.6 to 1.3 dB below 11.0 dBi, and its
narrow-plane beam is 2.3 to 4.8° wider than 23°. Over 9 to 11 GHz the spread
stays below 2 dB except at 9.0 GHz (2.06 dB).

## Run it

```bash
python scripts/showcase/design_lens.py --stage describe --out out/lens   # builds the three meshes, no solve
```

The design itself needs a GPU and runs in three stages, each reading the
previous stage's output folder and writing a new one:

```bash
python scripts/showcase/design_lens.py --stage trial --boundary nearest --meshes 1.0 --out out/lens-trial
python scripts/showcase/design_lens.py --stage main --start primary --boundary nearest --meshes 1.0 --record out/lens-trial --out out/lens-main
python scripts/showcase/design_lens.py --stage resolve --boundary nearest --meshes 1.0,0.75,0.5 --band 9,11,0.1 --record out/lens-main --out out/lens-resolve
```

The mesh is graded by default. `--boundary nearest` gives a cell that straddles
two lens pixels the permittivity of the pixel that holds its centre; on the
graded mesh every stage but `describe` refuses to run without a `--boundary`
choice. `main`
refuses to start unless the trial met its continuation rule; `resolve` solves
the final design again on the three meshes over 9–11 GHz in 0.1 GHz steps. Add
`--objective fan` to every stage for the fan beam.
